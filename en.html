The Art of Disguise

Why we believe (and don’t believe) hidden advertising

Introduction

A typical situation: you open Instagram and see a post from your favorite blogger. They talk about a new product, share a personal experience, and show how they use it in everyday life. The post doesn’t look like a familiar ad: there is no aggressive call to buy, no pushy slogans, no list of benefits. It all looks like an ordinary recommendation from someone you are used to trusting.

Yet behind this post there may be an advertising contract.

This is precisely what makes native advertising distinctive — a form of promotion that seeks to blend organically into the familiar environment of content consumption. Unlike traditional advertising, which openly invites you to buy a product or use a service, native integration uses the language, style, and format of the platform so that the commercial message is perceived as a natural part of the information flow.

Today, advertising is increasingly becoming part of stories, journalistic pieces, videos, podcasts, and bloggers’ personal recommendations. The line between editorial content, an author’s opinion, and commercial promotion is gradually becoming more complicated.

For brands, this is an opportunity to build a closer emotional connection with potential consumers. For audiences, it is an additional source of information that is not always easy to tell apart from independent content.

But a fundamental question arises: if advertising stops looking like advertising, does it become more persuasive, or does it, on the contrary, begin to undermine trust?

To make sense of this contradiction, it is important to look not only at the marketing mechanisms but also at the professional side of creating ad integrations, the specifics of digital media, and the behavior of the audience itself.

Chapter 1. From an Advertising Message to a Full-Fledged Story

In recent years, the way brands interact with audiences has changed significantly. Users no longer depend on a limited number of information sources. News, entertainment, reviews, and advertising posts all sit in one digital feed. People decide for themselves what content to watch, whom to trust, and what to pay attention to.

In these conditions, traditional advertising communication faces a problem: audiences have learned to ignore messages that they perceive purely as an attempt to sell them something.

Aizhan Mukasheva, a marketing director who has worked in PR and marketing since 2012, notes that the key difference of native advertising lies in its long-term nature.

According to the expert, traditional advertising directly offers a product for purchase, whereas native integration can gradually build interest and a desire to own the product that was seen. For example, a user notices a particular product in a post, remembers it, and some time later buys it on their own.

Such an effect is harder to measure, because a considerable amount of time may pass between exposure to the advertising message and the purchase. Moreover, a person’s decision is influenced by many other factors: personal needs, financial situation, and purchasing power.

Mukasheva also points out that the naturalness of an integration depends not so much on the format of the post as on the way the story is told.

Thus, the advertising message becomes part of a story rather than a separate commercial announcement. Instead of a direct list of product features, the audience is offered a context, a situation, and an experience through which they get to know the brand on their own.

However, it is important to distinguish between quality storytelling and simple ad disguise. Using conversational language, footage from everyday life, or personal stories does not in itself make an integration native. If the content of a post is entirely subordinated to an advertising script, the audience may recognize the artificiality of such a presentation.

Chapter 2. Why Does the Audience Trust Some Recommendations and Ignore Others?

One of the key factors in the effectiveness of native advertising is the personality of the person who delivers the advertising message.

In traditional advertising, the main source of persuasion is the brand itself: the company talks about the advantages of its product and explains why the consumer should choose it.

In influencer marketing, communication is built differently. An intermediary appears between the brand and the potential buyer — a blogger, journalist, expert, or another author whose opinion already carries a certain weight with the audience.

But a large number of followers does not in itself guarantee trust.

Aizhan Mukasheva stresses that the choice of blogger should depend on the goals of the advertising campaign. Some authors are better suited to building reputation, others to short-term promotion and generating sales.

In her words, what matters is not only reach but also the quality of the audience, its loyalty, its level of education, the blogger’s own reputation, and how well their values match those of the brand.

The expert makes an important observation: the same blogger may have a completely different audience on YouTube, Instagram, and Telegram. Therefore, it is wrong to assess the potential effectiveness of a collaboration solely by the total number of followers.

In the paper Marketing through Instagram Influencers: The Impact of Number of Followers and Product Divergence on Brand Attitude, the authors studied how an influencer’s number of followers and the fit between the advertised product and the influencer’s image affect the audience’s attitude toward the brand.

The results of two experimental studies showed that influencers with a large number of followers are perceived as more popular and, under certain conditions, are more likable. However, popularity does not always automatically mean a higher level of perceived opinion leadership.

Moreover, working with major influencers may not be the best choice for promoting products that differ greatly from the author’s usual image. In such cases, the perceived uniqueness of the brand declines, which may negatively affect the audience’s attitude toward it.

This is especially important for understanding native advertising: the effectiveness of an integration is determined not only by the size of the audience but also by how logical the product’s presence is within a particular person’s content.

If a blogger who has spent years building an image as an independent expert in a certain field suddenly starts advertising a product completely unrelated to their work, the audience may doubt the sincerity of the recommendation.

Therefore, long-term collaboration, shared values, and a natural connection between the author and the advertised product become important conditions for a quality integration.

Chapter 3. The Kazakhstani Context: Megawatts and Skepticism: How to Explain a Complex Industry to People Without Bureaucratese

The problem of trust is especially noticeable when promoting large companies, government organizations, and representatives of the quasi-public sector.

Unlike consumer goods advertising, corporate communication is often tied to more complex topics: infrastructure projects, energy, the economy, industry, and decisions of public significance.

How do you explain to an ordinary person the technical processes taking place at a large enterprise? How do you talk about large-scale construction so that the post doesn’t look like yet another corporate report?

These questions are especially relevant for organizations whose activities directly affect the lives of regions and their populations.

Damir Aidar, a journalist, PR specialist, media manager, producer, and screenwriter with 23 years of experience in journalism and PR, believes that the main task of corporate communications is to find the human story behind complex technical information.

As an example, he cites the construction of a 1,000-megawatt combined-cycle power plant in the Turkestan Region.

From the standpoint of technical reporting, one can regularly publish information about the number of cables laid, equipment tests, and the progress of installation work. However, for a broad audience, such details often remain incomprehensible.

In Damir Aidar’s view, it is much more important to explain why the region needs a new power plant, how it will help solve the problem of electricity shortages, and what significance the project will have for industrial development.

This approach shows that nativeness lies not only in the advertising form. It can manifest itself in the very way complex information is explained: through people, real processes, visual materials, and consequences that the audience can understand.

Aidar also points to the skeptical attitude of part of the audience toward communications from large government and quasi-government organizations. When corporate pages publish daily posts about meetings held, agreements signed, and successes achieved, readers naturally ask: what does all this have to do with my life?

That is why the expert suggests using a journalistic approach even when preparing corporate materials. One of his professional filters is to imagine yourself as the editor of an independent outlet and ask: would he himself take such a piece on, or throw it in the trash?

This is an important principle that separates informing from self-promotion. If a company has really solved a problem, built a facility, or created jobs, those facts can speak for themselves. Extra epithets such as “unique,” “large-scale,” and “historic” do not necessarily make a message more convincing.

According to Aidar, the audience notices fairly quickly when an ordinary event is being passed off as an outstanding achievement. In such cases, excessive self-praise can backfire — and reduce trust.

Chapter 4. When Nativeness Becomes Manipulation

Native advertising has a contradiction that cannot be ignored: the more natural an ad integration looks, the harder it is for the user to tell that they are looking at a commercial message.

At first glance, this is an advantage of the format. From the standpoint of media communication, however, an ethical problem arises: does the audience have the right to know that a post was created as part of a paid collaboration?

The study by Bartosz Wojdynski and Nathaniel Evans, Going Native: Effects of Disclosure Position and Language on the Recognition and Evaluation of Online Native Advertising, published in 2016 in Journal of Advertising, addresses this problem directly.

The authors conducted two experimental studies on how the placement and wording of a disclosure affect the recognition of native advertising.

The results showed that placing the label in the middle or lower part of the material, as well as using wording containing the words “advertising” or “sponsored,” increased the likelihood that users would recognize the material as advertising. At the same time, recognizing the commercial nature of a post was generally accompanied by a more negative evaluation of the material.

The invisible disclosure

  1. The label sits in the header. 7% of readers noticed it.
  2. The same label inside the text, mid-article.
  3. Ad recognition rose to 40% and more, nearly six times higher.

This finding is especially important. It shows that openly labeling advertising can change a user’s attitude toward a post, but the absence of a label is not proof of trust. On the contrary, if a person does not realize that they are looking at commercial material, their positive reaction may be based on an incomplete picture of the situation.

Here it is necessary to separate two concepts: trust in the content and trust in the source of information.

A user may agree with a blogger’s story, appreciate the quality of the material, and even become interested in the product. But if it later turns out that the recommendation was paid for and the author’s commercial interest was concealed, the attitude toward the blogger and the brand may change.

Damir Aidarov believes that a native approach does not release a company from responsibility for the transparency of its communication.

He also opposes commissioned posts containing knowingly false information, purchased comments, the artificial creation of public support, and attempts to discredit critics of the company.

Aidar calls the topics of ecology, safety, and workplace injuries especially sensitive. In his view, it is unacceptable to use bloggers to create a positive information background while a real problem remains unresolved.

Thus, the ethical boundary of native advertising runs not between traditional and modern forms of promotion, but between honest communication and the deliberate misleading of the audience.

Chapter 5. Storytelling, Video, and New Tools of Corporate Communications

One reason for the popularity of native advertising has been the development of digital formats. Today it is not enough for brands to prepare a single universal advertising piece and place it on every available platform.

Each digital platform implies its own model of information consumption.

Aidar stresses that the same news hook must be adapted to the specifics of each audience.

For example, for a business publication, a piece about the construction of a power plant may include investments, technical specifications, and project timelines. On Instagram or TikTok, dynamic shots, short explanations, and the stories of the people involved in the construction matter more. On YouTube, one can post a full-length documentary about the development of the facility.

At the same time, the expert believes that industrial PR still underuses the potential of video.

As an example, he describes a pilot eight-minute short fiction film created as part of a corporate project. Members of the youth council took part in the filming, and the power plant itself became a separate character in the narrative.

The idea was to present an industrial facility not through a traditional report or news story, but through a story with heroes and an emotional component.

At the time of the interview, the film had been shown only to an internal audience at a closed screening. The team plans to develop the project further and present it on social media.

This example demonstrates how far corporate communication can go beyond the classic press release.

However, Aidarov does not believe that traditional media and press releases are losing relevance. In his view, they continue to perform important functions but have stopped being the only way to engage with an audience.

Aizhan Mukasheva’s observations about the role of user-generated content — UGC — can be linked to this. Today, companies increasingly use materials that look like posts by ordinary users. But this format, too, is gradually becoming recognizable to audiences.

Therefore, the key factor remains not the particular technology of content production, but the persuasiveness of the story and how well it matches the audience’s interests.

Chapter 6. Can Trust Be Measured in Numbers?

Marketing and PR have a large number of performance indicators. Companies analyze views, reach, reposts, comments, engagement, click-throughs, and the number of target actions.

However, the number of interactions with a post does not always reflect a real change in the audience’s attitude toward the brand.

He calls measuring reputation and trust one of the most difficult tasks in corporate PR.

In his view, a large number of views does not yet mean that a communication campaign has succeeded. It is necessary to understand what exactly the audience took away from the material, how correctly it understood the message, and whether its attitude toward the company has changed.

As an example, the expert suggests considering publications about the construction of the power plant. If, after the materials come out, journalists, local residents, and business representatives begin to better understand the significance of the project, its progress, and its potential benefit to the region, the communication achieves its goal.

At the same time, Aidar stresses the importance of regular studies of awareness and trust levels. Only this way can changes be tracked and results compared, rather than drawing conclusions solely from comments on social media.

Aizhan Mukasheva looks at effectiveness from a somewhat different angle. In her professional practice, she focuses on CPV (Cost Per View) — the cost of a single view — and, when specific target actions are needed, uses CPL (Cost Per Lead) — the cost of acquiring one potential customer.

These indicators help assess the economic efficiency of an advertising campaign, but they do not give a complete picture of trust.

Thus, it makes sense to consider the effectiveness of native advertising on several levels:

Four levels of effectiveness

  1. 1Media levelreach, views, impressions, and frequency of exposure to the post.
  2. 2Behavioral levelclick-throughs, interactions, sign-ups, requests, and purchases.
  3. 3Reputational levelthe audience’s attitude toward the brand, the perception of its credibility, and willingness to recommend it.
  4. 4Informational levelthe audience’s understanding of the content of the post and of the product itself.

It is especially important not to conflate these indicators. A view is not trust, a comment is not loyalty, and a purchase does not necessarily mean a positive attitude toward the brand.

Native advertising can influence a consumer’s decision after some time, so its result does not always show up immediately after publication.

Chapter 7. Trust in an Age of Information Overload

The development of digital platforms has led to a situation in which audiences encounter an enormous number of advertising and informational messages every day. Brands compete not only with one another but also with news, entertainment videos, personal posts, and independent channels.

In these conditions, trust becomes one of the most significant resources of digital communication.

Damir Aidar believes that in the coming years the Kazakhstani media market will continue to develop toward short videos, independent channels, and podcasts. Artificial intelligence, which is already used for writing texts, translation, editing, and processing large volumes of information, will have a significant influence on content production.

However, as the volume of content grows, so will the difficulty of verifying information.

In the expert’s view, this is exactly why professional journalism will not lose its importance. The ability to check facts, analyze documents, work with different sources, and ask the right questions will remain an important advantage for a journalist.

This observation bears directly on the future of native advertising. The more commercial messages there are in the digital space, the greater the audience’s need for reliable information and transparent sources.

At the same time, it cannot be claimed that every user perceives advertising influence in the same way. Aizhan Mukasheva points out that purchasing decisions depend not only on marketing tools but also on economic circumstances, personal needs, and purchasing power.

As an example, she cites consumer behavior in periods of economic instability: some people may buy small items or visit cafés more often because larger purchases become less affordable.

Consequently, the influence of advertising cannot be considered in isolation from the socio-economic context.

Even a well-prepared integration does not guarantee a purchase. It can generate interest, remind people of a product, or influence the attitude toward a brand, but the final decision depends on a combination of factors.

Conclusion. Naturalness Must Not Become a Way to Deceive

Native advertising has become an important part of modern digital communication. It allows brands to go beyond the traditional advertising message, use storytelling, collaborate with opinion leaders, and adapt information to the specifics of different platforms.

However, its effectiveness is determined not only by the quality of the content or the popularity of the author.

The practical observations of Damir Aidarov and Aizhan Mukasheva complement these conclusions with professional experience in journalism, PR, and marketing.

Both experts, despite their different professional positions, draw attention to the need for the advertising message to match the audience’s interests. For one, this means finding the real story behind corporate information; for the other, choosing the right blogger, integration format, and way of engaging with the consumer.

It is especially important that native advertising must not replace independent journalism or create an illusion of public support. Its naturalness should rest on the quality of the storytelling, not on the concealment of commercial interest.

Ultimately, trust cannot be built by one successful ad, a large number of followers, or high view counts. It is formed from consistent actions, reliable information, and open interaction between the brand and the audience.

Perhaps the main paradox of native advertising is this: to earn the audience’s trust, a brand must give up trying to look as though it isn’t advertising at all.

Because natural communication is not one in which the user fails to notice the advertising, but one in which they understand its origin and still consider the message worth their attention.
